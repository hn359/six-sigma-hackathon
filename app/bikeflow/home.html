About

A control-chart dispatcher for bikeshare vans

Bikeshare stations run empty or full at rush hour, and the rebalancing van is often sent blindly. This tool watches every station at every reading. It sends the van when a station behaves unusually for that time of day or leaves its healthy range, and it plans how many bikes each station should start with the next morning.

User: the dispatcher who runs one rebalancing van and a reserve depot of spare bikes.

The tabs

  • Map and dispatch: the live map, its time bar, and how the dispatcher should act on what it shows.
  • End-of-day redistribution: the 10 PM plan that sets every station for the 6 AM opening.
  • SPC charts: each station's day and its control chart, and the statistics behind them.

Data

Where the data comes from

Our simulator (data/simulate.py) plays out a small bikeshare city with a fixed random seed, so every file can be rebuilt exactly.

  1. Riders: at each reading, the number of rentals at a station is a Poisson draw with a rate for that station and time of day (higher at rush hour).
  2. Trips: each rider rides to another station, picked with weights for that time of day: homes to campus and offices in the morning, back in the evening.
  3. Limits: an empty station loses the rental; a full one sends the rider on to the emptiest station with room.
  4. Three files per city: week 1 with no van (the baseline), week 2 with the van running our rules, and an event day with two planted problems (a slow drain and a crowd) to test the chart.

Limits

Known limits

  • Simulated data only; demand rates are invented and tuned, not fitted to a real system.
  • One van, one stop per reading, no travel time or van capacity.
  • Same demand every day: no weekends, weather or semester effects.
  • The van's limits, the overnight plan and the rush hours are learned once from week 1 and never updated; only the charts' limits roll forward daily.
  • Rush hours use net-flow activity, not rentals (the readings have no rental counts).

For the dispatcher

How the dispatcher should act

  1. Pick a city and a day on the map: Event day (a day with planted problems), Week 2 · policy (the van runs on our rules) or Week 1 · no van (the baseline), then a weekday.
  2. Press Play or drag the time bar. One reading per step; the speed menu sets how fast. Amber bands under the bar mark the rush hours, and a Rush hour badge shows while the replay is inside one. The end-of-day and SPC tabs follow this bar.
  3. Read the map. Each station card shows bikes docked out of its docks and its status (in control, or the rule that fired). The ranked list on the right puts the station that needs the van most at the top; the van drives to the stop it makes.
  4. Check the sections under the map: 1 Station status (how full each station is now), 2 Rider flow (which routes riders took) and 3 Redistribution (which bikes the van should move now, from where to where).
  5. Act on the flags as in the table below.
Flags

How a reading gets flagged, and what it changes

FlagWhat it meansEffect on the dispatcher's choice
Empty or full, now or projected within the hour riders cannot rent or cannot park: an operational problem, whether or not it is unusual HIGH priority in 3 Redistribution; send the van first
Outside the healthy range (below 20% or above 80% of the docks) the station is drifting toward empty or full MEDIUM priority; also triggers the van on the map as a safety net
Control-chart signal (a red dot in the SPC charts) rider flow is unusual for that time of day, compared with recent normal days: something the usual pattern does not explain, such as an event, a crowd or a broken dock MEDIUM priority; on the map, rules 1–4 send the van toward the station's expected level

A MEDIUM station is raised to HIGH when it was empty or full in the coming hour on at least half of the comparable past days: a recurring problem, not a one-off.

The van

What the van does at every reading

  1. For each station, compare its rider net flow with what is usual for that time slot.
  2. The station triggers if a control-chart rule fires, or if it is outside 20%–80% of its docks (a safety net for slow drifts).
  3. The van moves the station back toward its expected level: at most 10 bikes, one stop per reading, the station furthest from its expected level first.
  4. Bikes come from (or go to) the station with the most to spare on the other side, then the reserve depot.
End of day

Redistribution at the end of each day

At closing, the dispatcher gets a plan that the van finishes by 10 PM, so every station opens at 6 AM with the right number of bikes for the morning rush. It is 1 Tomorrow's bike plan above.

1. Decide each station's starting level

  1. Learn the morning pattern from week 1. For each station, average its rider net flow from 06:00 to 10:00 over the van-free baseline week. Negative means riders mostly leave it (homes); positive means they mostly arrive (campus, offices).
  2. Turn that into a share of the docks (the 80/20 rule):
    • the station riders leave most starts at 80% of its docks, so it has bikes to rent;
    • the station riders arrive at most starts at 20%, so it has room to park;
    • every other station sits in proportion between: from 50% up toward 80% for departures, from 50% down toward 20% for arrivals, by how large its flow is next to the biggest one;
    • a station with no clear pattern (or no history) starts at 50%.
  3. Start = share × docks, rounded. Collegetown, 20 docks each: S01 Cascadilla 15, S02 Schwartz 4, S03 Dryden 16, S04 College Ave 8, S05 Eddy St 9.

2. Plan the van's moves

  1. Compare each station's bikes at closing with its start: above it must give bikes, below it must receive.
  2. Rank by how many bikes must move: HIGH = 6 or more, MEDIUM = 3–5, LOW = fewer.
  3. Match, highest priority first: a station that needs bikes takes them from stations that must give, then from stations with spare bikes (never below their own start), then from the depot. Bikes a station still has to give after that go to the depot.
  4. Check: each bike moves at most once, no station goes below 0 or above its docks, and docked bikes + depot always equals the fleet.

The van's moves: S02 → S04, 2 bikes and S03 → S04, 1 bike (S04 first, it is the MEDIUM one); S01 → S05, 1 bike; S03 → depot, 1 bike. Five bikes moved, and every station opens on its start.

The plan uses week 1's averages, not a forecast, and it is only published once all seven week-1 mornings are complete; before that the tool shows that it is still learning. It keeps using week 1 afterwards, so week 2's results never change the rule they are testing. With no depot count, it assumes every bike not docked at closing is at the depot. No travel time or van capacity is modelled.

Statistics

The statistics

WhatHow
Chart pointrider net flow in a slot (change in bikes minus the van's move) minus the usual net flow for that slot over the baseline week. This removes the normal rush-hour drain, so only unexplained variation is charted.
Control limits (I-MR)σ = MR̄ / 1.128; limits = centre ± 3σ; moving-range UCL = 3.267 MR̄. The charts use the last 7 clean days before the charted day, updated daily: readings beyond 3σ are removed and the limits recomputed until none remain (the course's revised limits). A switch on the chart shows week 1's fixed limits for comparison.
Rules that send the vanWestern Electric rules 1–4 (beyond 3σ; 2 of 3 beyond 2σ; 4 of 5 beyond 1σ; 8 in a row on one side), or the station outside 20%–80% of its docks. The van judges these against week 1's fixed limits, so the simulated data and van log stay valid.
Rules on the BikeFlow chartthe course's eight tests plus a moving range above its limit. These are shown for analysis; the van currently acts on rules 1–4 above, so a dot below can appear without a van visit.
Weekly reviewa p-chart of each day's share of out-of-range readings: week 1 sets p̄ and the limits, week 2 days are judged against them.
Rush hoursa slot is rush when the bikes riders move in or out of all stations is at least 1.5× the day's average (week 1); rush slots less than an hour apart are joined. Collegetown: 07:00–09:15 and 16:00–18:30.

Assumptions: chart points roughly independent and normal; readings within a day are correlated, so limits are a little tight; seven baseline days per slot average.

Reading the charts

What a red dot means

  • One flag means look; repeated flags mean act. On ordinary week-2 days the chart flags about 6–9% of readings with the eight tests (8–13% with the moving-range test), more than the 0.3% that 3σ suggests, because bike counts are small and each reading depends on the last.
  • A sudden spike (test 1, or a moving-range jump) usually means an event or crowd: send the van now.
  • Several readings in a row off to one side (tests 2–3) mean a drain or surge building: go before it empties or fills.
  • A long one-sided run or a steady trend (tests 4–5) suggests a lasting change, such as broken docks or construction: rebalancing only hides it, so consider a repair crew.

These are guidance for the dispatcher; the tool does not yet choose a different action per test.
